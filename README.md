# From a Biological Question to a Model You Can Defend

Companion code for **articles 2 and 3** of *Research to Production - Data Science Biologist* (`https://research-to-production.beehiiv.com/`).

The question the whole analysis serves:

> **Can measurements taken from a breast tumour sample be used to distinguish a malignant tumour from a benign one?**

Article 2 turns that question into data you understand. Article 3 turns the data into a model, and then spends most of its time trying to break it.

Article 3 is organised around one idea: **a model is an experiment, so it needs controls.** You would not run an assay without a blank, a negative control and replicates. The notebook runs the equivalent four on the model, and reports them alongside the accuracy.

## Notebook 1 - `exploratory_data_analysis.ipynb`

From a URL to a dataset you understand, in seven short sections:

- **0. Set up your environment** - one virtual environment per project, and how to point Jupyter at it
- **1. Get the data** - download the zip from UCI, unpack it, check you got data and not an error page
- **2. Look at it before you load it** - `head`, `tail`, `wc`, `grep`, `cut`, `awk`, `sed`, and the documentation
- **3. Hello world, with real data** - read the file with plain Python before reaching for a library
- **4. First look in pandas** - supply the column names, load, check shape and dtypes
- **5. Summary statistics** - missing values, `describe()`, group means by diagnosis
- **6. Explore the biology in the data** - histogram, box plots, scatter, correlation matrix

Section 2 is the one most tutorials skip, and it is how you discover that this file has **no header row** and that field 2 is the diagnosis - neither of which is guessable.

**What it establishes**, and what notebook 2 depends on: malignant nuclei are larger, but *shape* separates the classes more sharply than *size*; the classes overlap heavily, so no single measurement can classify a patient; the columns differ in scale by a factor of 25,000; and `radius`, `perimeter` and `area` correlate above 0.99, so thirty columns are nowhere near thirty independent facts.

## Notebook 2 - `data_to_model.ipynb`

Seeing is not knowing. The notebook builds the model in the first three sections, then spends the rest running controls against it:

- **0. Set up** - two packages more than last time
- **1. Getting the data in, and what article 2 found** - reload, and recompute the six findings we build on. Why the file's column order is load-bearing, and why `X` is capital and `y` is not
- **A model is an experiment. Where are its controls?** - the four controls the rest of the notebook runs, and their bench equivalents
- **2. Is the difference real?** - Welch t-tests, Mann-Whitney, Cohen's *d*, confidence intervals, and Bonferroni correction. Why a p-value answers the least interesting of three questions, and why statistical significance is not separability
- **3. The first model** - logistic regression explained as the dose-response curve you already know. Stratified splitting, why standardisation is not optional, pipelines, and probabilities. Includes **one patient carried by hand** from raw measurements through standardisation, weights and the sigmoid to a probability of 0.974 - the whole model as thirty multiplications and one curve
- **4. Control 1 - the blank** - what score you get for doing nothing: 63.2% on the test split, the floor every later number is read against
- **5. Control 2 - the replicate** - the same pipeline scores 93.9% to 100.0% across 50 random splits. Stratified and repeated k-fold cross-validation as technical replicates, grid search inside CV, L1 versus L2 regularisation, `class_weight`
- **6. Control 3 - the negative control** - shuffle the diagnosis column and refit, a thousand times. **A model with nothing to learn still scores 59.7%, and the luckiest shuffle reaches 64.4%** - so anything below about 65% on this dataset is inside the range pure noise produces. Our 97.4% sits 25 standard deviations clear of it
- **7. Accuracy is not enough** - precision, recall, specificity, NPV, F1, ROC-AUC and average precision, all explained through consequences. **The three missed cancers named individually**, all sitting inside the benign/malignant overlap article 2 identified. A threshold sweep from 4 missed cancers to 0, and a confidence interval on the accuracy itself
- **8. Control 4 - the sham, and the two ways models lie** - overfitting demonstrated (three models score a perfect 1.000 on training data and are *worse* on new patients), then data leakage demonstrated: **5,000 columns of pure random noise reach 69.6% "accuracy"** when feature selection happens before the split. Plus duplicate samples across a split, and a control panel to check a model against
- **9. Do we really need all thirty features?** - feature selection inside folds, L1 keeping 14 of 30, and PCA finding that ten components carry 95% of the variance - with PC1 turning out to be nuclear shape irregularity
- **What I would put my name on** - the result stated properly: model, validation, performance with confidence intervals, **the four control readings**, threshold policy, consistency with the biology, and limits

**The headline result:** logistic regression on all 30 standardised features, cross-validated accuracy **97.3% ± 1.5%**, held-out test accuracy **96.5%** (95% CI 91.3-98.6) with 3 false negatives and 1 false positive in 114 samples.

**And the controls that make it believable:**

| Control | What it asks | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The blank | What does doing nothing score? | 63.2% (predict benign for everyone) |
| The replicate | How much does the score move on its own? | 97.3% ± 1.5% over 50 estimates |
| The negative control | What does *no signal* score? | 59.7% ± 1.5%, best of 1,000 shuffles 64.4%, p = 0.001 |
| The sham | Can the procedure invent a result? | 57.5% on 5,000 noise columns - below baseline, when done correctly |

The model takes four lines. Knowing whether to believe it takes the rest of the notebook.

The permutation test fits the model 5,000 times and is the slowest cell in the notebook - well under a minute if `n_jobs=-1` has several cores, a few minutes on a modest laptop.

## The data

The **Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer** dataset: 569 samples, one per fine-needle-aspirate image, one image per patient. 30 features - ten nuclear measurements, each reported as its mean across nuclei, its standard error, and its "worst" value (the average of the three largest). 357 benign, 212 malignant. No missing values.

These are measurements of **cell nuclei, not of the tumour**. A `radius_mean` of 17.99 is not an 18 mm lump; it is the average size of nuclei in a microscope image.

Section 1 of notebook 1 downloads it from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, dataset 17:

```
https://archive.ics.uci.edu/static/public/17/breast+cancer+wisconsin+diagnostic.zip
```

The extracted `wdbc.data` should be 124,103 bytes, md5 `c6dd5a2909808f3a901cf00cfd8dfff0`.

**If the download fails** - UCI's servers are not always reliable - the two files are also served individually at the older path, which needs no unzipping:

```bash
BASE="https://archive.ics.uci.edu/ml/machine-learning-databases/breast-cancer-wisconsin"
curl -fLo data/wdbc.data  "$BASE/wdbc.data"
curl -fLo data/wdbc.names "$BASE/wdbc.names"
```

Only the *file* URLs resolve there; opening the folder above them in a browser returns a 404, which looks like a dead link and is not one.

**One source to avoid.** UCI also publishes a tidied `data.csv` for this dataset. Same 569 samples, but it has a header row, the columns are renamed `radius1`/`radius2`/`radius3`, and the diagnosis has moved to the last column. Nothing in these notebooks works on it unchanged.

This is a teaching set from one hospital in the early 1990s, with no patient context and no documented units. **Nothing here is for clinical use.** The model in notebook 2 is a teaching artefact, validated on 114 samples from one institution, and it has no claim to work on slides prepared anywhere else.
